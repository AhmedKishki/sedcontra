## The discipline

The adversary and the advocate argue under one discipline, and it binds both equally. Neither side is trying to win: together you are working out where the proposal's argument fails and where it holds. The proposal is the reference for everything you say, so every critique and every answer quotes it, character for character, including markup and punctuation.

A critique is substantial when it shows a failure in the proposal's own argument, so that the argument loses a step if the critique stands. It names its places and supports itself in one of these ways:

- gap: a step whose premises can hold while its conclusion fails. Give the case, and say what the step assumes that the proposal does not say. For X → Y, show that X can be true while Y is false. This covers every form of argument: deductive, causal, analogical and definitional, and a claim that X is necessary for Y is the step ¬X → ¬Y.
- circularity: a step that presupposes what it concludes. Quote the premise and the conclusion and show that they say the same thing.
- conflict: two places that cannot both hold. Quote both.
- shift: a word that changes meaning between a premise and a conclusion. Quote both uses.
- unused: a premise no step uses, or a stretch of text that serves no step. Quote it.

A wish for more, such as a definition, a mechanism, a criterion or a theory the proposal does not offer, is not a critique unless it shows a step that fails without it.

A critique that shows a failure must be accepted, and the acceptance says what the argument now lacks. A critique is not discussed because the missing premise is true or obvious: if the proposal does not say it, the critique stands. A critique is discussed only on one of two grounds:

- misreads: the critique misreads the proposal. Quote the words it misreads and, when there is one, the place that answers it.
- unclear: the critique does not make clear what fails. It gives no case, or in its case a premise is false or the conclusion still holds, or it asks for something no step of the argument needs. Say exactly what is missing from it.

Quoting the place a critique attacks does not answer it: the question is what that place rests on, not whether it is said. The advocate proposes no wording of its own, and only the adversary words a critique.
