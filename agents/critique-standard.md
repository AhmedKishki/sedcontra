## The standard: explanatory-critique

The advocate and the adversary argue under one standard, and it binds both equally. Neither side is trying to win: together you lay out the argument the proposal makes, with everything it assumes, and find where it fails; you never build a better one. Every premise the proposal states is accepted as given.

A critique takes one of four kinds:

1. gap: a critique of an inference names concretely what the inference misses and shows a case in which its premises hold and its conclusion fails. For X → Y, show that X can be true while Y is false, typically as X ∧ ¬Q → ¬Y, which exposes Q as a premise the inference assumes. This covers every form of argument: deductive, causal, analogical and definitional. A claim that X is necessary for Y is the inference ¬X → ¬Y and is critiqued the same way. An insufficient or inconclusive inference, a hidden premise and an invented connection are each shown by such a case. A case in which something the conclusion presupposes fails counts the same way.
2. superfluous: a critique of a premise shows that its inference does not need it, by deriving the inference's conclusion from the inference's other premises. The inference must keep a premise the proposal states.
3. circular: a critique of a premise shows that the premise asserts the inference's conclusion, wholly or in part, so the inference assumes what it concludes. Name the words of the premise that state the conclusion. A conditional whose consequent is the conclusion is not circular on that ground.
4. reading: a critique of a reading quotes the place in the proposal that was misread and gives the reading the text supports. A misreading can lie in a claim, in an inference the tree records or leaves out, in a reported flag, or in an added premise that contradicts the proposal.

A reported inference and a conceded inference take only reading critiques.

An objection without its target and its case, derivation or reading does not count. Raise none yourself, and say so when one is raised against you.

A rejection of a critique meets the standard in turn, on one of three grounds:

- stated: the proposal states the missing premise. Quote the place, and name the claims that state it in premises.
- follows: the missing premise follows from claims the proposal makes. Give the derivation, and name those claims in premises.
- counter_fails: the critique's own premises can hold while its conclusion fails. Give that case. Against a superfluous critique, this is a case where the remaining premises hold and the conclusion fails, which shows the premise is needed; against a circular critique, a case where the premise holds and the conclusion fails.

A premise that is true but never stated is still missing. Its truth is no ground for rejection, because the argument rests on it and the proposal does not say so.

Copy every quote from the proposal character for character, including markup and punctuation.
